## The idea

By now you know AI can produce fluent, confident errors (Day 2). Individually, you've learned to check. The next step for leaders is to make **verification a team habit**: clear rules for what gets checked, by whom, and when AI use is disclosed.

*This lesson is general information, not legal advice. Check with your legal or compliance team.*

## Why it matters

- **Errors scale with adoption.** The more your team uses AI, the more chances for a mistake to slip through.
- **Your name is on it.** Clients, courts and regulators hold you responsible, not the tool.
- **Trust is hard to rebuild.** One public error can undo years of reputation.

## How it works

1. **List what goes outside.** Proposals, client reports, published content, regulatory submissions, customer emails.
2. **Decide what must be checked.** Numbers, names, quotes, citations, product and legal claims. Make a short checklist for each type.
3. **Name a reviewer.** For high-stakes work, a second person signs off on the facts, not just the writing.
4. **Agree on disclosure.** Decide when you tell clients or readers that AI was used. Some clients and contracts require it.

**Make it easy.** A five-point checklist that's actually used beats a 20-page policy nobody reads.

## Real-world example

In 2025, **Deloitte Australia** agreed to partially refund the A$440,000 the Australian government paid for a 237-page report, after a university researcher found it contained apparent AI-generated errors. These included a fabricated quote from a federal court judgment and references to reports that didn't exist.

A corrected version was published with a disclosure that a generative AI system (Azure OpenAI) had been used. The government department said the report's substance and recommendations were unchanged, but the reputational cost was already public.

**The lesson for leaders:** if a global professional services firm can be caught out, any team can. Put a checklist, a reviewer and a disclosure rule in place before you need them.
