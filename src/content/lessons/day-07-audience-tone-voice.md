## The idea

AI writes in a pleasant, generic voice by default: polished, slightly wordy and recognisably "AI". To get writing that lands, give it three things: **who's reading**, **how it should feel**, and **how you sound**.

## Why it matters

- **People skim generic messages.** Customers and colleagues tune out text that could have been written for anyone.
- **The same facts need different framing.** A board wants risks and numbers first; a frontline team wants "what changes for me".
- **Brand consistency.** Without guidance, ten people prompting the same tool produce ten different voices.

## How it works

1. **Name the audience.** Role, what they care about, how much time they have: "a CFO at a mid-size manufacturer, five minutes, sceptical of hype".
2. **Set the tone.** Nielsen Norman Group describes four dimensions: funny–serious, formal–casual, respectful–irreverent, and enthusiastic–matter-of-fact. Pick a point on each that fits.
3. **Show your voice.** Paste two or three samples you're proud of and ask the AI to describe your style as rules. Edit them into a **voice card**: five short rules plus words to avoid.
4. **Reuse it.** Paste the voice card into every writing prompt, or save it in your assistant's custom instructions or a project (more on that on Day 10).

## Real-world example

**Mailchimp** publishes its content style guide online. It sets out a clear voice (plain-spoken, genuine, with dry humour) and explains how tone shifts by situation: lighter in a welcome email, serious in an error message.

That's exactly the kind of guidance AI needs. Teams are now pasting short versions of their style guides into AI prompts so every draft starts on-brand.

**The lesson for leaders:** if your team or brand has a voice, write it down in a few rules. It helps new hires and AI alike, and turns AI drafts from "close" into "ready".
